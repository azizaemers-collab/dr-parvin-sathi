I became a doctor because I wanted to save lives. I stayed in obstetrics because I understood that the lives most at risk were the ones least likely to be saved — not for lack of medicine, but for lack of access.

During my internship, I stood in a room at 2:30 in the morning and watched a young mother and her unborn child die from eclampsia — a condition that is almost entirely preventable with a drug that costs less than one dollar. The medication existed. The knowledge existed. The system to deliver both of them did not.

"That night changed everything. I realized my career wasn't just about medicine; it was about systems."

I am now a postgraduate trainee in Obstetrics and Gynecology at a hospital in Bangladesh, where I see the consequences of the maternal health delivery gap every week. I see it in women who arrive too late because there is no 24-hour emergency support at the local level.

The Bridge to Global Health

What drives my career is the belief that the solutions developed in low-resource settings like mine are not only applicable in Bangladesh. They are directly transferable to under-resourced communities in the United States — where maternal mortality rates in states like Louisiana and Mississippi rival those of lower-middle-income countries. My mission is to adapt clinical protocols that solve the delivery gap regardless of geography.

By focusing on implementation science and task-shifting, we can bridge the gap between global medical standards and the reality of bedside care in both developing nations and American healthcare deserts.
